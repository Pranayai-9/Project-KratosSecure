1) Normal (OPD/IPD) flow
India – normal flow
Registration: Front desk captures basic demographics, phone, sometimes Aadhaar/ABHA; small fee; patient ID created; insurance often checked later or not at all for OPD.
linkedin

Encounter: High-volume OPD; token-based queuing; short consults (5–10 min); vitals may be taken by a nurse/assistant; prescriptions dominate.
linkedin

Documentation: Variable—paper prescriptions to EMR notes; IPD has admission notes, daily notes, nursing charts, discharge summary; hybrid paper+digital common.

Lab/Radiology: Doctor writes order; attendant carries slip to lab/radiology; samples collected; reports as print/PDF; LIS/RIS present in bigger hospitals but interfaces uneven.

Billing: Central billing counter; OPD pay-per-service; IPD advance deposit + interim bills + final bill; TPA/insurance and government schemes add steps; cash/UPI dominant.
linkedin

Discharge (IPD): Doctor writes discharge summary; nurse coordinates bed clearance; patient clears dues; discharge slip + prescriptions + follow-up date; actual exit often delayed by billing/insurance.
linkedin

Foreign (US/UK) – normal flow
Registration: Demographics + insurance/NHS number verified up front; eligibility and pre-authorization checked (especially US); consent forms signed.


Encounter: Lower volume, longer consults; structured history/exam/plan; formal triage more common in specialty clinics; shared decision-making emphasized.


Documentation: Comprehensive EHR notes, problem lists, medication reconciliation; IPD has detailed care plans, nursing flowsheets; documentation tied to coding (US).

Lab/Radiology: CPOE from EHR; electronic routing to LIS/RIS; barcoded specimens; results auto-posted to EHR with critical-value alerts.

Billing: US: complex revenue cycle (eligibility → coding → claim → denial management); UK: simpler per-visit billing for private care, NHS funding for public care.

Discharge (IPD): Early discharge planning; medication reconciliation; scheduled follow-ups before discharge; summaries sent to PCP/specialists; patient portal used for instructions.

2) Emergency (ED) flow
India – emergency flow
Emergency/arrival: Mostly walk-ins; some ambulances; initial “who looks worst” screen by nurse/security; care may start before formal registration.


Registration: Separate counter; family provides details; insurance/TPA often captured later; many cases start as self-pay; registration can lag behind clinical care.

Triage: Formal tools (ESI/Manchester) in some corporate/teaching EDs; many still use informal red/yellow/green or token order; documentation may be minimal.


Resource allocation: Manual bed hunting by charge nurse/resident; limited monitored beds/ventilators; corridor care common; surge protocols inconsistent.


Staff coordination: Charge nurse/senior resident coordinates via phone/WhatsApp with wards, radiology, ICU; physical or basic digital tracking boards.

Treatment: High load per clinician; orders may be verbal/paper; attendants move samples/reports; turnaround times variable; protocols exist but adherence varies.



Disposition: Decide discharge/observe/admit/transfer; admission often delayed by bed scarcity and billing/TPA clearance; follow-up usually just a date on a slip.


Foreign (US/UK) – emergency flow
Emergency/arrival: Strong EMS integration; pre-arrival alerts, vitals, ECGs; life-saving care not delayed for registration; bedside registration common.
pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih

Registration: Often at bedside after triage; US ties registration to insurance eligibility and co-pay; UK verifies NHS number; admin steps designed not to block care.

Triage: Standardized 5-level systems (e.g., ESI in US, Manchester in UK); structured fields in EHR (level, vitals, chief complaint, resource estimate).
ahrq

Resource allocation: Formal bed management systems, dashboards, and command centers; surge protocols (overflow areas, ambulance diversion) more systematically triggered.

Staff coordination: NIC and EPIC roles run huddles; electronic whiteboards show patient status, tasks, and disposition plans; clear escalation to hospital command center.

Treatment: Protocol-driven care (sepsis, stroke, trauma) with EHR order sets; CPOE standard; automated result routing and decision support; time targets monitored.

Disposition: Clear admit/discharge/transfer decisions; major focus on reducing “boarding” time for admitted patients; structured discharge education, follow-ups, and handoffs.
